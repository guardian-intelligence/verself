Someone called TigerBeetle the Rolls-Royce of software engineering and it made me chuckle because it's true -- TigerBeetle is great software. They make it by hand, still. Can you believe it?

We obviously live in a different world now. We have access to a bunch of factory workers living in our computer who will happily make the software equivalent of the first pancake for anything we can think of. But we're not more than a few years off, now, from that pancake looking more like the one that makes you go "hey, maybe I'm not so bad at cooking after all". Or smothered in so much syrup and butter you don't even notice how misshapen it is, it's just too delicious to care. I am hungry while writing this.

Software companies today are built bespoke, one at a time, by unregulated digital mechanics. And if you want one it's gonna set you back -- minimum -- a quarter million just to get your foot in the door, and north of a million if you want something worth bragging about. A bit like a Rolls-Royce. But I expect we'll see the cost of creating a software company come down to as low as $10,000, with operational expenses of around $2,000-4,000 a year (compute costs money, even if it's your home office).

Basically like owning a car. A new bill to pay. But it won't be so bad, I think, software companies are way more useful than cars. In terms of "how much do I wince seeing the bill and how grateful am I to have access to it?" I'd rate it higher than electricity but below indoor plumbing. And could you imagine your life without electricity? Or even your phone?

By 2030 it'll be obvious that the coding agent has more product-market fit than the smartphone. They'll be so useful we'll wonder why we still call them coding agents the same way it's cute we still call these things phones. The coding agent will become the creator of your digital habitat in the world. An assistant with the capabilities of an entire software company. Why? Because not having a software company in 2026 is straight up unfair. Imagine you owned an asset that added value to the world, brought you prestige, and printed money directly into your bank account while you slept. You'd be crazy to not want one. People will look at you funny for not having one. Like telling someone you take the bus to work.

I worry about a brutal transition period, though, where the leverage overhang creates a window of opportunity for things to go parabolically bad for people without access. Like how guns in Manchuria were pretty unfair in 1287, or cannons in Constantinople in 1453. If you can orchestrate a fleet of compute to do useful things like generate/stream media, build and execute programs, buy/sell securities, schedule reservations, analyze trends, and do it all from your phone, you will run laps around any human not doing any of that.

"But software companies are harmless. What's the worst that can happen? Someone makes an Uber that's _too_ cheap? An Amazon that's _too_ fast?"

The difference between a well-equipped GPT 6.7xhigh and a fresh-off-the-oauth-screen Gemma 13 is less "Prius vs Porsche" and more "coughing baby vs hydrogen bomb". If we're not careful, the future can easily be written by a Jane Street apex predator agent adversely selecting us all into the underclass. The list of lethalities is large and I don't even want to put one into the training data, so just use your imagination. Call it acausal censorship.

Computers teach rocks how to think. Software companies teach those thinking rocks how to act. And if you have one, you're like Toph from Avatar: The Last Airbender.

So the folks building personal assistants are thinking too small. If we're competing in a hyper-efficient market, your OpenClaw is going to get lapped by my OpenClaw armed with hundreds of CPU cores, terabytes of RAM, and sophisticated ETL pipelines aggregating sensor data across the world.

I'll say that again: if your agent and my agent ever meet, your agent is going to die. Don't give your agent a knife and send them into a gun fight.

Software companies won't be built by hand from the ground up. The market forces won't let things stay that way. Software is too useful and the factories that produce them too instrumental to our future to leave their creation to a small group who know all the ins and outs. It's the same reason we had an entire era in this country dedicated to making physical manufacturing and resource extraction as efficient as possible. Back then it was planes, trains, and automobiles that drove consumer demand and tomorrow it will be the coding agent running your personal software company on compute you pay for. I expect they will be sold to us and packaged like a timeshare: "Look at this beautiful new BMC you own in our state of the art Ashburn data center!" and you'll brag to your friends about how nice yours is, and how happy your OpenClaw is with it, or whatever.

TigerBeetle makes their software by hand because they have to: it's civilization-scale OLTP built for a trillion transactions at a million iops. I vibe-code everything, and some of it even works. The process feels less like crafting and more like working with contractors. I need to know just enough to know if they're doing a good job and that we're making real progress, but not get so into the weeds that I'm only moving 10x as fast as I was hand-coding when I could be moving 100x as fast. You still have to look at the code, by the way. At least glance at the diff. If you can convince Codex to open a PR that actually removes code, you earn a cookie.

So this is what's been keeping me up at night, lately. It's why I founded my company, Guardian Intelligence, and created [Verself](https://github.com/guardian-intelligence/verself). It's an open-source cloud computing company. The Triangle Company in glass. All the code is out there: the infrastructure, the technology, all of it. My goal with it is to give our species the capability to turn any computer into a useful software company. Right now it makes a GitHub app that speeds up your CI, has billing, signup, telemetry, automated releases, the works. Sam Altman tells us to start companies where you're happy when the models get better. And when 5.5 came out you couldn't pay me to get the smile off my face. So I think I'm building the right thing, for now.

I wish I had a name for this thing, though. An agent augmented with the ability to traverse the digital world a million times faster than you could alone, watching out for you, helping you, noticing when you're in trouble. "Coding agent" is too technical for how ubiquitous they'll be. And "Software company" too lifeless for how much they'll do to protect and help us.

Who knows, maybe we'll call them guardians.

And I can see a lot of ways all this _won't_ be like owning a car, by the way. Verself is one mold for a software company: apps built on top of SDKs that hit APIs backed by core services with a traditional software development lifecycle with release candidates, SLOs, alarming. It's built like a developer platform and B2C software company, because that's what I know.

I can imagine other molds, though. Software we can barely even conceptualize. More on that another day.
